 
 
UNIT I: UNDERSTANDING GENDER 
1. Introduction: Definition of Gender 
Meaning of Gender 
Gender refers to the socially and culturally constructed roles, responsibilities, behaviours, 
and expectations assigned to women, men, and other gender identities. 
Unlike biological sex, gender is created by society and therefore differs across cultures and 
historical periods. 
Definitions 
According to Towards a World of Equals: 
Gender refers to the socially constructed differences between women and men rather than 
biological differences. 
According to Judith Butler: 
Gender is not something we are born with; it is something we continuously perform through our 
actions, behaviour, language, and interactions. 
Key Characteristics 
• Gender is socially constructed. 
• Gender varies across cultures. 
• Gender roles change over time. 
• Gender creates unequal power relations. 
• Gender affects education, employment, family life, and politics. 
 
Difference Between Sex and Gender 
Sex Gender 
Biological Socially constructed 
Determined at birth Learned through society 
Universal Varies across cultures 
Cannot easily change Can change over time 
Male/Female Masculinity/Femininity and other identities 
Example: 
A baby is born male (sex). 
Society teaches him: 
• Don't cry. 
• Be brave. 
• Earn money. 
These expectations are gender. 
 
2. Basic Gender Concepts and Terminology 
Understanding gender requires familiarity with important concepts. 
Sex 
Biological differences between males and females. 
Examples: 
• Chromosomes 
• Hormones 
• Reproductive organs 
 
Gender 
Social expectations about how men and women should behave. 
Examples: 
• Girls should cook. 
• Boys should earn. 
These are social expectations, not biological facts. 
 
Gender Identity 
A person's internal understanding of their gender. 
Someone may identify as: 
• Woman 
• Man 
• Transgender 
• Non-binary 
• Genderqueer 
 
Gender Expression 
How a person expresses gender through: 
• Clothing 
• Hairstyle 
• Voice 
• Behaviour 
• Body language 
 
Gender Roles 
Responsibilities assigned by society. 
Examples 
Women: 
• Cooking 
• Child care 
• Household work 
Men: 
• Breadwinner 
• Protector 
• Decision maker 
These roles differ across societies. 
 
Gender Equality 
Equal rights, opportunities, responsibilities, and treatment regardless of gender. 
Examples 
• Equal education 
• Equal salary 
• Equal political participation 
 
Gender Equity 
Providing fair opportunities according to different needs. 
Equality means giving everyone the same. 
Equity means giving support according to need. 
Example 
Scholarships for girls in rural areas promote gender equity. 
 
Patriarchy 
A social system where men possess greater power than women. 
Characteristics: 
• Male dominance 
• Property controlled by men 
• Leadership mostly by men 
• Women's freedom restricted 
 
Feminism 
A movement seeking equal rights for women. 
Objectives: 
• Equality 
• Justice 
• Freedom 
• End discrimination 
• Women's empowerment 
 
Gender Stereotypes 
Oversimplified beliefs about men and women. 
Examples 
• Boys don't cry. 
• Girls are weak. 
• Women are emotional. 
• Men are natural leaders. 
These stereotypes create inequality. 
 
Gender Discrimination 
Unfair treatment because of gender. 
Examples 
• Unequal pay 
• Denial of education 
• Workplace discrimination 
 
Gender Bias 
Gender bias is a preference or prejudice for or against a particular gender that results in unequal 
treatment. 
Gender Roles 
Gender roles are the social expectations and responsibilities assigned to individuals based on 
their gender. 
Empowerment 
Empowerment is the process of enabling individuals, especially those who are disadvantaged, to 
gain confidence, rights, opportunities, and control over their lives. 
Inclusion 
Inclusion means ensuring that all individuals, regardless of gender or other differences, are 
respected, valued, and given equal opportunities to participate. 
Diversity 
Diversity refers to the presence and acceptance of differences among people, including 
differences in gender, culture, age, religion, and abilities. 
Gender Justice 
Gender justice is the fair and equitable treatment of all people, ensuring that no one faces 
discrimination or inequality because of their gender. 
Femininity 
Definition: Femininity refers to the set of qualities, characteristics, behaviors, and roles that a 
society traditionally associates with women and girls. These traits are socially and culturally 
constructed and may differ across cultures and time periods. 
Examples of traits associated with femininity: 
Caring and nurturing 
Compassion 
Emotional sensitivity 
Gentleness 
Cooperation 
Patience 
Examples: 
A mother caring for her child with love and affection is often considered an example of 
femininity. 
A teacher showing empathy and kindness toward students demonstrates feminine qualities. 
A woman choosing to wear traditional clothing like a saree or a dress may be viewed as 
expressing femininity, depending on the cultural context. 
Note: Both women and men can display feminine qualities. 
Masculinity 
Definition: Masculinity refers to the set of qualities, characteristics, behaviors, and roles that a 
society traditionally associates with men and boys. Like femininity, masculinity is socially and 
culturally constructed and varies across different societies and historical periods. 
Examples of traits associated with masculinity: 
Courage 
Confidence 
Independence 
Leadership 
Assertiveness 
Physical strength 
Examples: 
A firefighter bravely rescuing people during an emergency demonstrates qualities traditionally 
associated with masculinity. 
A team captain confidently leading and motivating teammates is often seen as displaying 
masculine traits. 
A father taking responsibility for protecting and supporting his family may be viewed as 
expressing masculinity. 
Note: Both men and women can display masculine qualities. 
Key Point for Exams 
Femininity and masculinity are gender roles shaped by society and culture, not fixed biological 
characteristics. 
Their meanings vary across cultures, historical periods, and social contexts. 
Modern gender studies emphasize that individuals can express both feminine and 
masculine traits regardless of their sex

These attitudes influence: 
• Behaviour 
• Education

• Marriage

Positive Attitudes

• Equal opportunities 
• Shared household work

Negative Attitudes

Sources of Gender Attitudes 
Family 
Children first learn gender expectations at home. 
Examples: 
Girls: 
• Help mother 
• Stay indoors 
Boys: 
• Play outside 
• Become independent 
 
School 
Schools reinforce gender through: 
• Textbooks 
• Teachers 
• Sports 
• Classroom activities 
 
Religion 
Religious interpretations sometimes reinforce gender inequality. 
 
Media 
Television and films often portray: 
Women: 
• Beautiful 
• Caring 
• Emotional 
Men: 
• Strong

4. Construction of Gender 
Gender is socially produced rather than biologically fixed. 
According to Judith Butler, 
Gender is repeatedly created through: 
• Speech 
• Clothing 
• Gestures 
• Behaviour 
• Social expectations 
She calls this Gender Performativity. 
Meaning: 
People perform gender every day without realizing it. 
Example 
A boy avoids crying because society expects boys to be strong. 
This repeated behaviour creates masculinity. 
 
Social Institutions Construct Gender 
Family 
Teaches different responsibilities. 
School 
Different expectations for boys and girls. 
Religion 
Creates moral expectations. 
Media 
Shows ideal women and men. 
Workplace 
Creates unequal opportunities. 
 
Judith Butler's Theory of Gender Performativity 
One of the most influential ideas in Gender Studies. 
Main arguments: 
• Gender is not natural. 
• Gender is produced through repeated acts. 
• Society rewards "acceptable" gender behaviour. 
• People who challenge gender norms are often criticized. 
Example 
A woman wearing trousers was once considered unacceptable. 
Today, society accepts it. 
This shows gender norms change. 
 
5. Gender Socialization 
Definition 
Gender socialization is the lifelong process through which society teaches individuals how to 
behave according to gender expectations. 
 
Agents of Gender Socialization 
Family 
First teacher of gender roles. 
School 
Different treatment by teachers. 
Peer Groups 
Friends influence behaviour. 
Religion 
Shapes moral values. 
Media 
Influences beauty standards. 
Workplace 
Maintains gender hierarchy. 
 
Effects 
Positive 
• Social identity 
• Cultural belonging 
Negative 
• Inequality 
• Gender violence 
• Career restrictions 
• Mental pressure 
 
6. Making Women 
This chapter in Towards a World of Equals explains how society "creates" women through 
socialization. 
Girls are taught: 
• Be obedient. 
• Be modest. 
• Serve family. 
• Avoid risks. 
• Prepare for marriage. 
They receive: 
• Dolls instead of toys. 
• Cooking lessons. 
• Household responsibilities. 
Result 
Women often become economically dependent and socially restricted. 
 
Preparing for Womanhood 
Society prepares girls for adulthood by emphasizing: 
• Marriage 
• Motherhood 
• Domestic work 
• Beauty 
• Modesty 
Restrictions include: 
• Limited mobility 
• Dress codes 
• Curfews 
• Protection of family honour 
The textbook argues these expectations reduce women's freedom. 
 
7. Making Men 
Society also creates masculinity. 
Boys are expected to be: 
• Strong 
• Brave 
• Independent 
• Competitive 
• Emotionally controlled 
Common messages 
• Boys don't cry. 
• Be a man. 
• Never show weakness. 
These expectations create pressure and emotional problems. 
 
Problems Created by Masculinity 
• Mental stress 
• Aggression 
• Violence 
• Emotional isolation 
• Fear of failure 
The textbook explains that patriarchy harms men as well as women. 
 
8. Growing Up Male 
This chapter discusses how boys experience gender socialization. 
Boys learn: 
• Leadership 
• Independence 
• Dominance 
• Risk-taking 
They are discouraged from: 
• Crying 
• Housework 
• Expressing emotions 
This creates unrealistic expectations of masculinity. 
 
9. First Lessons in Caste 
This chapter explains how caste and gender are interconnected. 
Children first learn caste through: 
• Family 
• Religion 
• Marriage customs 
• Food practices 
• Friendships 
Girls often face double discrimination because of: 
• Gender 
• Caste 
Dalit women experience multiple forms of oppression. 
 
Relationship Between Gender and Caste 
Examples 
• Restrictions on inter-caste marriage. 
• Honour killings. 
• Occupational discrimination. 
• Educational inequality. 
The textbook argues that caste and patriarchy work together to maintain social inequality. 
 
Judith Butler's Contribution to Gender Studies 
Her major contributions include: 
• Gender is socially constructed. 
• Gender is performed through repeated acts. 
• There is no single natural identity. 
• Society creates gender norms. 
• Gender can be challenged and transformed. 
Her theory has influenced: 
• Feminism 
• Queer Theory 
• Gender Studies 
• Cultural Studies 
• Literary Criticism 
 
Important Points for Examination 
• Gender is a social construction, while sex is biological. 
• Gender roles are learned through socialization. 
• Family, school, media, and religion shape gender identity. 
• Patriarchy creates unequal power relations. 
• Gender stereotypes reinforce discrimination. 
• Judith Butler's concept of gender performativity explains that gender is created 
through repeated social actions. 
• Towards a World of Equals shows how society constructs femininity and masculinity 
through everyday practices. 
• Gender and caste often intersect, producing multiple forms of inequalities.
